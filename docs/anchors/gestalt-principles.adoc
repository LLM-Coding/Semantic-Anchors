Full Name:: The Gestalt principles of perceptual grouping -- the factors by which people see separate elements as belonging together, described by Max Wertheimer in 1923 and applied today in visual and interface design

Also known as:: Gestalt laws, laws of perceptual organization, principles of grouping

[discrete]
== *Core Concepts*:

* *We see wholes before parts.* The eye groups elements into units without effort. Design either works with this grouping or fights it.
* *Wertheimer's factors.* In "Untersuchungen zur Lehre von der Gestalt II" (1923) Max Wertheimer named proximity, similarity, uniform destiny (common fate), objective set, direction, closure, good curve, good Gestalt and past experience.
* *Proximity.* Elements close to each other read as a group. In interfaces, spacing alone can tie a label to its field.
* *Similarity.* Elements that share colour, shape or size read as related.
* *Closure and common fate.* The eye completes incomplete shapes, and elements that move together read as one unit.
* *The factors compete.* Aurora Harley of NN/g notes that proximity often overrides similarity. A design reads correctly only when the factors agree.
* *The list grew in design practice.* The NN/g study guide also covers common region -- elements inside a shared border read as a group -- which is not among Wertheimer's original factors.

Key Proponents:: Max Wertheimer ("Untersuchungen zur Lehre von der Gestalt II", _Psychologische Forschung_ 4, 1923, pp. 301–350; English: https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Wertheimer/Forms/forms["Laws of Organization in Perceptual Forms"], translated by W. Ellis in _A Source Book of Gestalt Psychology_, 1938); Kurt Koffka and Wolfgang Köhler as co-founders of the Berlin school; in UX practice the Nielsen Norman Group (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/visual-design-in-ux-study-guide/[visual design study guide], 2022; Aurora Harley, https://www.nngroup.com/articles/gestalt-proximity/["Proximity Principle in Visual Design"], 2020)

[discrete]
== *When to Use*:

* Reviewing a form or screen where labels, fields and buttons do not visibly belong together
* Explaining why a layout feels confusing, in terms that point at a fix: more space here, a shared border there
* Teaching visual hierarchy to developers and writers
* Asking a model to review a UI: with the anchor, it explains defects through named grouping laws, proximity first

[discrete]
== *Common Misunderstandings*:

* *Expecting one canonical list.* Wertheimer's factors differ from the lists in design books; common region, for instance, came later.
* *Applying the principles as independent rules.* They compete, and proximity often wins over similarity.
* *Reading them as explanations.* They describe grouping tendencies; they do not explain why perception works that way.
* *Dropping "Principles" from the name.* In our prior test on 2026-10-28, the bare "Gestalt" resolved to psychology on every tier and brought up Gestalt therapy. With "Gestalt Principles", six of six runs explained the defects of a signup form through at least three laws, proximity in all six; no baseline named one.

[discrete]
== *Criticism*:

* *Laws with little precision.* Wagemans, Elder, Kubovy, Palmer, Peterson, Singh and von der Heydt review a century of research in https://ruccs.sas.rutgers.edu/images/archive/personal-manish-singh/papers/Wagemans_etal_2012_I.pdf["A century of Gestalt psychology in visual perception: I. Perceptual grouping and figure–ground organization"] (_Psychological Bulletin_ 138(6), 2012). They report the long-standing charge that classic Gestalt work offered demonstrations instead of explanations, "formulating laws with little precision".
