:definition: One-Way Door / Two-Way Door Decisions is a distinction from Jeff Bezos's 2015 letter to Amazon shareholders: a one-way door (Type 1) decision is irreversible or nearly so and deserves slow, careful deliberation, while a two-way door (Type 2) decision can be undone and should be made quickly by individuals or small groups. Its purpose is to match the weight of the decision process to how hard the decision is to reverse. In software it is used to mark which changes can be rolled back and which cannot, such as a dropped column or a published API.

====
Full Name:: One-way door and two-way door decisions, from Jeff Bezos's 2015 Letter to Shareholders of Amazon.com, Inc.

Also known as:: Type 1 / Type 2 decisions; reversible and irreversible decisions; one-way and two-way doors

[discrete]
== *Core Concepts*:

One-Way Door (Type 1):: A decision you cannot walk back. Bezos: "Some decisions are consequential and irreversible or nearly irreversible – one-way doors – and these decisions must be made methodically, carefully, slowly, with great deliberation and consultation."

Two-Way Door (Type 2):: A decision you can undo. Bezos: "But most decisions aren't like that – they are changeable, reversible – they're two-way doors." If the result is poor, "You can reopen the door and go back through."

Match the Process to the Door:: The letter argues against "one-size-fits-all" decision making. Type 2 decisions "can and should be made quickly by high judgment individuals or small groups". The heavy process is reserved for Type 1.

The Named Failure Is Slowness:: Bezos's target is the large organisation that uses "the heavy-weight Type 1 decision-making process on most decisions, including many Type 2 decisions", which ends in "slowness, unthoughtful risk aversion, failure to experiment sufficiently, and consequently diminished invention".

The Opposite Failure Is Fatal:: The letter's footnote covers the reverse error: "Any companies that habitually use the light-weight Type 2 decision-making process to make Type 1 decisions go extinct before they get large."

Classify First, Then Decide:: The working question comes before the decision itself: can this be undone, by whom, and at what cost? In a code change the usual one-way doors are deleted or migrated data, a published API or event schema, a sent message, and anything a third party starts to depend on.

[discrete]
== *When to Use*:

* Stating in a pull request whether the change can be undone, so a reviewer can tell reversible changes from irreversible ones
* Deciding how much review, sign-off and rollout care a change deserves
* Architecture decisions: record in the ADR which consequences are hard to reverse
* Delegating: two-way doors go to the person closest to the work, one-way doors get consultation
* Unblocking a team that deliberates at length over a choice it could simply try and revert
* Next to the question of who is affected if the change is wrong: reversibility and reach are two separate facts about a change

[discrete]
== *Common Misunderstandings*:

* ❌ "Two-way door means the decision needs no thought" -- it means a lighter process, not none
* ❌ "A decision is one kind of door forever" -- a schema is cheap to change with an empty table and expensive with a full one; reclassify as dependants and data accumulate
* ❌ "A possible bug makes it a one-way door" -- what counts is whether the effect can be undone, not whether the change can fail
* ❌ "It is a complete review checklist" -- it sorts changes by reversibility and says nothing about correctness, accessibility or security
* ✓ "It decides how much process a decision gets" -- accurate, and that is the use Bezos describes
====

== *Criticism*:

* *Reversibility decays.* Saeed Habibi argues that the door type is not a fixed property: "Two-way doors can become one-way doors while you're not paying attention." His example is a schema decision that "became effectively permanent" once the table had grown, and his replacement question is about cost: "The question isn't whether you can reverse a decision. It's what reversal actually costs." https://dev.to/saeedhbi/reversibility-decays-what-bezoss-framework-misses-about-technical-decisions-4kna[Habibi, "Reversibility Decays: What Bezos's Framework Misses About Technical Decisions", DEV Community] (posted 3 February; the page shows no year).
* *People misclassify in both directions.* Habibi: "Engineers treat irreversible decisions as reversible, and reversible decisions as irreversible." Ozan Varol names the cautious half of the error: "Most decisions that initially appear to be irreversible are actually two-way doors masquerading as one-way doors." https://ozanvarol.com/what-most-people-miss-about-making-decisions/[Varol, "Most people miss this step when making a decision"] (21 May 2019).
* *It is not a licence for haste.* Shane Parrish restates the limit that the short form loses: "Reversible decisions are not an excuse to act recklessly or be ill-informed". https://fs.blog/reversible-irreversible-decisions/[Parrish, "Reversible and Irreversible Decisions", Farnam Street] (23 April 2018).
* *No academic or journal critique of the binary was found.* The named critiques are practitioner articles in English. That is a gap in our search, not in the discourse.

== *Current Status*:

* *The source is the 2015 shareholder letter, and it says "doors" in the plural.* The passage stands in the section "Invention Machine" of https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000119312516530910/d168744dex991.htm[Bezos's 2015 Letter to Shareholders] as filed with the SEC. The letter speaks of "one-way doors", "two-way doors", "Type 1" and "Type 2" decisions. We found no citable earlier use of the door metaphor by Bezos or Amazon.
* *Bezos repeated it a year later, tied to decision speed.* The https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000119312517120198/d373368dex991.htm[2016 letter] says: "Many decisions are reversible, two-way doors. Those decisions can use a light-weight process." The same passage adds the advice to decide with "somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had" and the phrase "disagree and commit".
* *Amazon now teaches it as a mental model and applies it to architecture.* AWS calls it "a mental model we call one-way and two-way doors" in https://aws.amazon.com/de/executive-insights/content/how-amazon-defines-and-operationalizes-a-day-1-culture/[Daniel Slater's "Elements of Amazon's Day 1 Culture"]. The https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/framework/the-review-process.html[AWS Well-Architected Framework] asks for reviews "early on in the design phase to avoid one-way doors that are difficult to change".
* *In software the criterion drifted from "irreversible" to "costly to reverse".* thoughtbot defines it by cost: "If the cost (financial, time, resources) of reversing a decision is high, it falls into the One-way door category". https://thoughtbot.com/blog/one-way-vs-two-way-door-decisions[Bireke, "One-way vs Two-way door decisions"] (10 May 2024). Google's Abseil performance tips apply it to technical properties: "Broadly speaking, fine-grained flag changes are easy to undo and thus two-way doors", and "The possibility of a bug does not make the door one-way." https://abseil.io/fast/87[Kennelly, "Performance Tip of the Week #87: Two-way doors"] (16 October 2024, updated 17 November 2025).
* *Reversibility as an architecture concern is older than the door.* Martin Fowler wrote in 2003 that an architect's task includes "finding ways to eliminate irreversibility in software designs". https://martinfowler.com/ieeeSoftware/whoNeedsArchitect.pdf[Fowler, "Who Needs an Architect?", IEEE Software] (July/August 2003). The door metaphor names the idea; it did not introduce it.
* *A prior test on 2026-11-23 measured what naming it changes, and what it hides.* Asked to assess four changes to an online shop, 12 of 12 runs with the term noted that a new public API field is hard to take back once published; without the term only the strong tier did. The term also narrowed the view: for a button-colour change, the weak and mid tiers mentioned contrast or accessibility in 8 of 8 runs without the term and in 0 of 8 with it. Use it next to other review lenses, not instead of them. See the link:#/prior-tests[prior-test register].
