= Blast Radius

:tier: 2
:definition: Blast Radius is an established term of software reliability and security for the extent of what is affected when something goes wrong: a failure, a compromise or a bad change. Borrowed from explosives and without a single originator, it asks who and what is exposed, and it leads to measures that contain the damage, such as cells, bulkheads, separate accounts, least privilege and progressive rollouts. In a pull request it states who or what is affected if the change is wrong.

[%collapsible]
====
Full Name:: Blast Radius, the extent of impact of a failure, a compromise or a change

Also known as:: scope of impact, impact radius; its upper bound is set by the failure domain or fault-isolation boundary

[discrete]
== *Core Concepts*:

Extent, Not Likelihood:: Blast radius answers "how far does it reach if it goes wrong?", not "how likely is it to go wrong?". A change can be unlikely to fail and still have a large blast radius. Google's _Building Secure and Reliable Systems_ gives the working definition: "Controlling the blast radius means compartmentalizing the impact of an event, similar to the way compartments on a ship grant resilience against the whole ship sinking."

One Idea, Several Causes:: The same question is asked of a failure (which customers lose service), of a compromise (what an attacker reaches from one stolen credential), of a deployment (how many hosts get the bad build) and of a code change (which callers, services and data it touches).

Failure Domains Set the Upper Bound:: A failure domain is the most an event can reach, not what it does reach: a process, a cell, an availability zone, an account, a tenant. A fault confined to one tenant does not affect the whole cell around it. The smallest boundary that actually holds caps the blast radius. AWS states the arithmetic for cells: "If a workload uses 10 cells to service 100 requests, when a failure occurs, 90% of the overall requests would be unaffected by the failure."

Containment in Architecture:: Bulkheads and cell-based architectures, shuffle sharding and separate accounts per workload or data domain keep an event inside one compartment.

Containment in Security:: Least privilege and segmentation limit what one compromised identity can reach. Microsoft's Zero Trust guidance puts it this way: least privilege "limits the blast radius of a potential breach so that if an account is compromised, the potential damage is limited."

Containment in Change:: Canary releases, progressive rollouts and feature flags expose a change to a few before all. Microsoft: "You can minimize the potential blast radius of deployment-caused issues by adopting a progressive exposure deployment model." HashiCorp gives the same reason for splitting Terraform state: "it is best to keep the potential blast radius of your operations small."

Containment in Experiments:: "Minimize Blast Radius" is one of the Principles of Chaos Engineering: the experimenter must "ensure the fallout from experiments are minimized and contained."

[discrete]
== *When to Use*:

* Stating in a pull request who or what is affected if the change is wrong: which services, tenants, consumers and data
* Reviewing a change to a shared library, a shared configuration or a schema with many dependants
* Planning a rollout: how many users or hosts see the change first, and what stops it from spreading
* Designing isolation: accounts, cells, tenants, queues, permissions
* Scoping the permissions of a service account, a token or an AI agent
* Sizing a chaos experiment or a risky migration
* Setting how much investigation or review a change deserves; `chestertons-fence` scales its effort the same way

[discrete]
== *Common Misunderstandings*:

* ❌ "A small diff has a small blast radius" -- a one-line change to a shared default can reach every consumer
* ❌ "Blast radius is the same as risk" -- it is the extent half of risk; likelihood is a separate question
* ❌ "Isolation removes the blast radius" -- it bounds it, and shared components such as routers, control planes and global configuration sit outside the boundary
* ❌ "Smaller is always better" -- every further compartment costs capacity and operations
* ✓ "It names who is exposed if this goes wrong" -- accurate, and that is the lift the prior test measured
====

== *Criticism*:

* *The metaphor is military, and a naming body recommends replacing it.* The Inclusive Naming Initiative lists "blast-radius" in its Tier 3 word list: "Recommended to replace." Its reasons are origin and clarity: "The term _blast radius_ comes from warcraft. It refers to the extent of damage caused by an explosion." It adds that the term "is not universally known within the IT community" and suggests "extent" or "affected components". https://inclusivenaming.org/word-lists/tier-3/blast-radius/[Inclusive Naming Initiative, "blast-radius"] (page undated).
* *Reducing it has a price.* Linhua Tang's SREcon24 EMEA talk carries the slide title "The art of balancing blast radius and cost" and says of cell-based architecture: "Definitely NOT for small scale systems". The costs named are infrastructure ("can you split 100GB cache into 1GB per cell?") and operations ("how to manage one hundred clusters instead of one"). https://usenix.org/system/files/srecon24emea_slides-tang.pdf[Tang, "Blast Radius Reduction for large-scale distributed systems", SREcon24 EMEA slides] (2024).
* *Shared components defeat the boundary.* AWS says so in its own best practice: "The router layer is a shared component between cells, and therefore cannot follow the same compartmentalization strategy as with cells." https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/latest/reliability-pillar/rel_fault_isolation_use_bulkhead.html[AWS Well-Architected, REL10-BP03].
* *No named source was found for two expected objections:* that the blast radius is hard to know in advance, and that the term blurs extent and likelihood. That is a gap in our search, not in the discourse.

[discrete]
== *Current Status*:

* *No originator, and no documented first use in computing.* https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blast_radius[Wikipedia] defines the physical sense as "the distance from the source that will be affected when an explosion occurs" and adds: "The term also has usages in computer programming." Neither it nor Wiktionary names a coiner or dates the move into computing. The catalog therefore lists `Community` as proponent and claims no coinage.
* *The vendors that spread the term often write "scope of impact" in their own text.* The AWS best practice is titled "Use bulkhead architectures to limit scope of impact", and the body of the Amazon Builders' Library article on shuffle sharding says "the scope of impact is reduced by the number of shards", while its page description says the technique serves "to limit blast radius". https://builder.aws.com/content/3F06NpJ8YeoIGP8VHTw4n81pFn8/workload-isolation-using-shuffle-sharding["Workload isolation using shuffle-sharding", Amazon Builders' Library] (5 December 2019). Expect both terms for one idea.
* *Microsoft's Zero Trust wording has moved.* The adoption framework still lists the principle "Assume breach" and describes it with "Minimize blast radius and segment access." https://learn.microsoft.com/security/zero-trust/adopt/zero-trust-adoption-overview[Microsoft, "Zero Trust adoption framework overview"] (updated 5 May 2025). The current overview renames it "Assume compromise (previously referred to as assume breach)" and places the term under least privilege: "reducing unnecessary exposure and potential blast radius." https://learn.microsoft.com/security/zero-trust/zero-trust-overview[Microsoft, "Zero Trust overview"] (updated 15 November 2026). A model's prior most likely holds the older wording.
* *Code-review tools now use it for pull requests.* CodeRabbit documents a feature of that name: "Security Blast Radius helps you investigate what a pull request could affect beyond its changed lines." https://docs.coderabbit.ai/security/blast-radius[CodeRabbit, "Blast Radius"]. Qodo's version "Estimates how broadly a failure caused by the change could affect the codebase or product functionality." https://docs.qodo.ai/code-review/assess-risk-with-blast-radius[Qodo, "Assess risk with blast radius"]; the same page says that a broader "Merge Risk" feature has since replaced it, with blast radius as one of its factors. Both are vendor documentation, and neither page shows a date.
* *Security and reliability share the term, and small models lean to security.* In the prior test of 2026-11-23 the bare term meant software operations or security on every tier, and explosives did not dominate; the weak tier leaned to cybersecurity, the mid and strong tiers to reliability and security. Say which event you mean: a failure, a compromise or a change.
* *The lift is moderate, hence tier 2.* On a task about a shared library that changes its default time zone for 14 services, answers with the term were organised by reach and more often said who is exposed. The risks themselves were found without the term as well. See the link:#/prior-tests[prior-test register].
